## S09-1: Dealer DMS "integration ransom" — dealers and vendors pay per-rooftop tolls to reach the dealer's own data (CDK/Reynolds duopoly)
- Who hurts: Franchised and independent auto dealers (1–50 rooftops) and the ~200+ third-party software vendors (CRM, scheduling, recon, marketing) who must reach DMS data; secondarily dealers trying to switch DMS.
- The pain: Every tool a dealer adds must be "certified" into CDK 3PA or Reynolds RCI; vendors pay $10k–$40k+ integration fees plus ~$200+/month per rooftop, and pass it to dealers, so a dealer pays a toll on every integration touching data the dealer owns. Dealers who try to leave get their data held: CDK sued its own customer (Asbury) when it tried to access its own data for a Tekion migration, and a Georgia court had to order the transfer. The June 2024 ransomware outage shut ~15,000 dealerships out of their own operating system for ~2 weeks.
- Frequency: Monthly per-rooftop fees on every third-party integration; per-vendor-added; per-DMS-switch (data hostage event); daily ops dependence (outage proved it).
- Economics today: Vendor-side integration fees $10k–$40k+ setup plus ~$200/mo/rooftop (DealerRefresh 2017 + 2026 community review); one dealer quoted $2,000 setup + $175/mo per location just to connect a CRM (4 locations = $8,000 + $700/mo); $630M settlement to a 243-company vendor class over inflated integration prices; $100M settlement to dealers over DMS overcharges; $1.02B direct dealer losses from the 2024 outage (Anderson Economic Group).
- Current solutions: Pay CDK 3PA / Reynolds RCI certified-integration tolls; a handful of authorized-but-costly data syndicators; switching DMS to Tekion/DealerBuilt/Dominion — which CDK litigates or slow-walks (Asbury injunction); Arizona-style dealer data laws (CDK lobbied against AZ HB2418). Sources: DealerRefresh threads, Tekion complaint, CBT News.
- Forcing function: Partial — no regulatory deadline; contract renewal cycles, litigation-won transfer rights (Tekion seeks a 10-day transfer injunction), and post-outage security reviews act as soft forcing. Honest label: mostly discretionary switching, but with documented money movement (settlement funds) and court-created leverage.
- Why now: 2024 outage broke trust ($1.02B losses); $630M (Jan 2025) and $100M (Aug 2024) settlements priced the abuse; Tekion's Dec 2024 federal antitrust suit and the Asbury injunction created legal precedent for data portability; DealerRefresh's May 2026 community review shows rage is current, not historical.
- Blocker risk (recorded per brief): CDK/Reynolds actively block uncertified access, litigate scrapers and rivals, and lobbied against data-access statutes — any third-party layer risks certification denial or suit; the same behavior is the pain.
- Evidence: see S09-1-E1..E6 in ledger (DealerRefresh #5220 2017-03; #5345 2017-07; #11705 community review 2026-05; CBT News $630M 2025-01; Tekion suit blog 2024-12; AEG $1.02B 2024-07).
- Scout's confidence: HIGH — decade-long complaint corpus + court records + priced settlements + 2026-current forum sentiment.

## S09-2: Community bank / credit union core lock-in — integration, conversion, and deconversion costs set by three vendors (Fiserv/FIS/Jack Henry)
- Who hurts: Community banks (<$10B assets) and credit unions — specifically CEOs/COOs/heads of ops and their IT leads; also the fintechs trying to sell into them.
- The pain: Top-3 cores serve 70%+ of banks and ~50% of credit unions; contracts are long, opaque, and priced à la carte, so every new digital product means a core integration the bank can't self-serve, and leaving means deconversion fees computed at the vendor's "then current rates." Banks told the OCC the commercial negotiating relationship is "very uneven"; ICBA's Jan 2026 letter names "legacy core system limitations, high integration and conversion costs, vendor concentration" as the modernization blocker.
- Frequency: Per contract cycle (5–7 years); per fintech/product integration; per M&A event (deconversion); ongoing monthly invoice complexity.
- Economics today: PYMNTS-reported complaint that ending a core contract can cost millions; a real SEC-filed example of $366k of deconversion fees expensed by one small holding company; deconversion priced by formula (e.g., trailing-12-month volume × months remaining) rather than a listed number (Godfrey & Kahn); banks hire negotiation consultants (Paladin fs, FTI) just to read the contract.
- Current solutions: Stay locked and buy the core's own add-ons; middleware/negotiation consultants; FIS's 2020 "simplified" community-bank contract (incumbent concession proving the complaint); comment letters asking OCC to examine vendors directly (ABA). Sources: American Banker, Banking Dive, OCC RFI, G&K.
- Forcing function: Partial — OCC scrutiny (RFI Nov 2025, comments closed Jan 27 2026) is pressure, not mandate; CFPB 1033 open-banking deadlines (would have forced data APIs from Apr 2026) are enjoined and being rewritten — honestly weakens the regulatory clock.
- Why now: The pain went onto the public record Nov 2025–Feb 2026 (OCC RFI + ICBA/ABA/AFC letters + Comptroller quotes) — buyer-side language is now quotable and organized; KC Fed 2024 documented the concentration.
- Blocker risk: Cores gate "certified" vendor programs and can price/slow third-party access; a wrapper layer sells into a risk-averse, examiner-supervised buyer — real friction for a solo founder (sales cycle risk noted for Manager 1).
- Evidence: S09-2-E1..E7 (Federal Register RFI 2025-11; American Banker 2026-02; Banking Dive 2026-04; ICBA letter 2026-01; G&K contract tips; PYMNTS 2019; SEC 10-K example).
- Scout's confidence: HIGH on pain reality/recency; MEDIUM on solo-founder attackability (buyer caution + vendor gatekeeping).

## S09-3: Dental practice legacy servers and hostage patient data (Dentrix/Eaglesoft on-prem)
- Who hurts: Solo and small-group dental practices (1–5 locations) — the dentist-owner and office manager; the local IT shops who serve them.
- The pain: The practice's clinical+billing record lives in a proprietary on-prem database (Dentrix pre-G5 used c-tree .dat files; images stored outside the DB with encoded filenames), so the practice pays mandatory monthly support to the vendor, pays again for eServices, and pays a third time to get its own data out when switching. Reports won't even export to Excel. Meanwhile the owner carries the server, backup, and HIPAA breach risk personally.
- Frequency: Daily dependence on the server; monthly support-plan billing; per-switch conversion event; per-audit/breach exposure.
- Economics today: $860 charged for "document conversion" on top of a switch (Capterra review); Open Dental publishes $1,450/database conversion price (US); mandatory paid monthly technical support (review); a paid data-liberation micro-industry exists (dataconversionprogrammer.com sells Dentrix extraction, "a few hours to several weeks"); post-migration rework from broken image links is the most common failure (SKF conversion guide).
- Current solutions: Stay on Dentrix/Eaglesoft + local MSP contract; Open Dental (open-source, still needs paid conversion); cloud PMS (Curve, Denticon, CareStack) with their own conversion projects; third-party extraction programmers. Sources: Capterra, Software Advice, Technibble, opendental.com.
- Forcing function: Partial — HHS's HIPAA Security Rule NPRM (Fed. Reg. 2025-01-06) proposes mandatory MFA/encryption that on-prem closets fail; it is proposed, not final (honest), but OCR enforcement and cyber-insurance questionnaires already bite today.
- Why now: Jan 2025 NPRM put a date-stamped compliance threat over every server closet; cloud PMS matured; 2024's healthcare-cyber incidents (vendor-side outages) made "the server under the desk" a board-level fear even in 3-chair practices.
- Blocker risk: Henry Schein/Patterson control the conversion tooling and certified add-on programs; database formats are undocumented on purpose — extraction layers live in tolerated grey space.
- Evidence: S09-3-E1..E7 (Capterra 2022-10 $860; Software Advice 2025-08 no-Excel-export; Technibble 2022-09 locked .dat; Open Dental pricing; HIPAA NPRM 2025-01; SKF guide; conversion-service page).
- Scout's confidence: HIGH — multiple independent practitioner artifacts + published prices + a regulator document.

## S09-4: Title agents forced off RamQuest — sunset deadlines plus per-file fee stack in title production systems
- Who hurts: Independent title agencies and small escrow/settlement shops (2–50 seats) running RamQuest (and E-Closing) — owners and production managers.
- The pain: Qualia bought RamQuest and E-Closing from Old Republic (Jan 2025) and set retirement clocks: ~1 year for RamQuest One (already passed, ~Jan 2026) and ~3 years for CCE (~Jan 2028). Agents must re-platform their entire production system, keep access to decades of historical files, and land on a modern TPS whose own users complain about add-on pricing and being charged per third-party item on top of a per-file fee.
- Frequency: One forced migration per agency (thousands of agencies) on a dated clock; then per-file and per-order fees forever; daily production dependence.
- Economics today: Qualia listed from $3,599/year (Capterra pricing page) plus per-file economics; reviews: "Add on pricing for a function I believe should be part of the base platform"; a Capterra review (surfaced via search index) alleging "Hidden fees, non refundable"; migration effort/data-archive risk called out by transition guides. Old Republic ran ~thousands of agent users (exact counts not public — labeled inference).
- Current solutions: Ride RamQuest CCE until sunset; take Qualia's discounted migration; move to SoftPro/ResWare; keep historical records in read-only archives or paper. Sources: ALTA, The Title Report, Premier One, Capterra.
- Forcing function: REAL — vendor-set sunset dates (RamQuest One ~Jan 2026 passed; CCE ~Jan 2028). A dated, unavoidable re-platform for an installed base.
- Why now: The clock started Jan 2025; the laggard cohort (small agencies without IT staff) is mid-scramble 2026–2028; consolidation removed the "do nothing" option.
- Blocker risk: The acquirer owns both the sunset and the destination — Qualia controls migration tooling and its marketplace charges per connected order; a third-party migration/archive layer competes with the acquirer's paid path.
- Evidence: S09-4-E1..E6 (ALTA 2025-01; The Title Report 2025-01; Premier One timelines 2025; Capterra reviews 2019/2025; Capterra pricing).
- Scout's confidence: MEDIUM — forcing function and dates are solid; verbatim agent-side rage is thinner than other candidates (agent forums are Facebook-gated; noted for Wave 2 to verify volume).

## S09-5: Distributors losing database access in the forced march to cloud ERP (Epicor Prophet 21 / Kinetic)
- Who hurts: Wholesale distributors ($5M–$500M revenue) on Epicor P21 or Kinetic — IT managers, ops leads, and the consultants who built their reports/integrations on direct SQL.
- The pain: On-prem P21/Kinetic shops built years of reports, Power BI models, and custom apps on direct SQL. Epicor's cloud prohibits direct SQL ("No, you cannot do SQL in cloud"); one user's fulfillment app polling the DB every 5 seconds would need ~500 BAQs to re-create, with ~300% REST overhead, and read-only replica access costs extra and requires being whitelisted by Epicare; on SaaS the user couldn't even find the data dictionary. Breakage risk is quantified: thousands in late-shipment fines if a sync fails during a 6,000-order Black Friday.
- Frequency: Continuous (daily reports/integrations); acute at each forced cloud migration; per-integration rebuild.
- Economics today: Read-only cloud DB is an extra-cost add-on (forum); rebuild effort measured in hundreds of BAQs (forum); API complexity pushes shops to paid consultants ("hundreds of fields... without an indication of whether the fields are required" — BizXcel, who then sells the fix); late-shipment fine exposure named by users.
- Current solutions: Stay on-prem while support lasts; pay for RO replica + ODBC with IP whitelisting; rewrite on BAQs/OData/CDC; hire Epicor-ecosystem consultants. Sources: epiusers.help threads, BizXcel, Epicor cloud FAQ.
- Forcing function: Vendor-driven — Epicor's cloud push and support windows force the migration event; no regulator. Label: partial (vendor-imposed deadlines, not law).
- Why now: The threads are Jan 2026 and Jun 2026 — this cohort is mid-migration now; identical dynamics run across Epicor Eclipse, Infor SX.e, and other distribution ERPs (pattern, one-vendor evidence — labeled).
- Blocker risk: Epicor gates DB credentials (whitelisting) and could close the replica path; building on it means building on a vendor's tolerance.
- Evidence: S09-5-E1..E4 (epiusers 2026-01; epiusers 2026-06; BizXcel 2025-01; Epicor P21 cloud buyer FAQ).
- Scout's confidence: HIGH — fresh, first-person, quantified; single-vendor concentration is the main caveat.

## S09-6: Freight brokers/carriers on green-screen TMS — expensive, closed, and re-keyed around (McLeod/TMW)
- Who hurts: Small asset carriers and freight brokerages (5–100 trucks / 5–50 broker seats) on McLeod LoadMaster/PowerBroker or Trimble TMW.Suite — owners, ops managers, settlement/billing clerks.
- The pain: The systems look and price like the era they were built in: "the system looks old like Windows 98"; "very very expensive... cost prohibitive to smaller companies"; support is a paid event ("Need help? You call, get billed for it"); TMW "didn't integrate with our accounting software... had to re-key data," so billing staff re-key settlements daily. Modern data tools (visibility, factoring, load boards) connect only through certified-partner programs.
- Frequency: Daily re-keying in billing/settlement; per-support-call billing; per-integration certification; monthly license cost pressure.
- Economics today: Pricing is quote-only (itself a complaint: "higher pricing with confusing cost structure," May 2025 review); support billed per call; a trained in-house key user is required staffing (Nov 2024 review); re-key labor in settlement/billing is a daily clerk-hours line (review). No public list prices — labeled honestly; Wave 2 should price via quotes/job posts.
- Current solutions: Stay and staff around it; bolt-on middlewares and certified partners (e.g., Highway's certified McLeod integration, Sep 2025); newer TMS (Tai, Turvo, Rose Rocket) for those willing to migrate. Sources: Capterra reviews, GlobeNewswire.
- Forcing function: none — discretionary (freight-recession margin pressure pushes cost scrutiny, but no deadline).
- Why now: 2023–2025 freight recession squeezed broker/carrier margins while legacy TMS costs stayed fixed; certified-integration ecosystems (2025 announcements) show demand for data paths the incumbents don't provide natively.
- Blocker risk: McLeod/TMW monetize certified integration programs; uncertified access is contractually shaky.
- Evidence: S09-6-E1..E6 (Capterra LoadMaster reviews 2018/2020/2023/2024; Capterra TMW.Suite reviews 2022/2025; Highway–McLeod PR 2025-09).
- Scout's confidence: MEDIUM — consistent multi-author review evidence, but forum-depth and hard dollar figures were unreachable this session (Reddit/TruckersReport blocked); economics partially qualitative.

## S09-7: Local-government permitting systems that stall contractors (Accela/Tyler EnerGov) — real pain, wrong buyer
- Who hurts: City permit-desk staff and, downstream, contractors/permit expediters whose projects stall; documented in SF, San Diego, NYC, Cape Coral.
- The pain: Implementations run late, over budget, and slow: SF's Accela rollout drew 53 builders/expediters testifying that waiting times got worse; Cape Coral halted permit acceptance for a backlog month on EnerGov and hired extra staff plus an integration consultant ("We underestimated how difficult this was going to be"); San Diego audited its Accela implementation to head off "project delays, cost overruns, or a failed implementation"; NYC's audit found validation/access-control deficiencies.
- Frequency: Daily permit operations; per-implementation (multi-year); per-project delay for contractors.
- Economics today: SF's $4.5M contract ran millions over with years of delay (SF Chronicle); Cape Coral added headcount (customer service, permit coordinators, EnerGov admin) plus a third-party integration consultant; contractor-side delay cost is real but unquantified here (labeled).
- Current solutions: Tyler EnerGov vs Accela duopoly persists (2026 comparison coverage); cities hire implementation consultants and staff around the system; contractors hire expediters — a services market that IS the workaround. Sources: SF Chronicle, San Diego audit, NYC Comptroller, Cape Coral Breeze, Civic IQ.
- Forcing function: none — discretionary procurement cycles.
- Why now: Honestly weak — freshest first-person artifact is 2022; 2026 coverage is secondary. Included because the pain artifacts are official (audits) and the contractor-side wedge overlaps ground 11 (permit expediting), where the buyer is private.
- Blocker risk + buyer risk: Government procurement is the anti-pattern buyer for a solo founder (18-month cycles); any wedge must sell to contractors/expediters, not cities. Recorded as the candidate's core weakness.
- Evidence: S09-7-E1..E4 (Cape Coral 2022-05; San Diego audit; SF Chronicle 2016; NYC Comptroller audit).
- Scout's confidence: LOW — pain is real and officially documented, but recency is stale, economics are secondhand, and the direct buyer fails the reachability rule; passing to Manager 1 mainly as contractor-side context for ground 11.
